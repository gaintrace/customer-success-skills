# Executive Engagement

> When to bring an executive into a save, how to brief them so the brief can be forwarded
> unedited, what the call must achieve, and the six ways it is botched.
>
> An executive is the most expensive and least renewable resource in a save. You get roughly one
> per account per year before the escalation path stops meaning anything, so the question is never
> "would an exec help?" — it is "what decision needs someone with authority the CSM does not have?"

## The ladder

Kristen Hayer's five steps, in order, with the explicit warning against jumping to step four `[P]`:

| Step | Action | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **De-escalate directly.** Ask about business impact and listen | "Until you understand the scope of the issue, you cannot effectively escalate it anyway" |
| 2 | **Tap peers.** A dedicated escalations channel, other post-sales teams | Contains the problem inside CS while you learn what it is |
| 3 | **Form a cross-functional group** — product, engineering, support, ops | Pre-decide who convenes it, who is in it, and what it is authorised to do |
| 4 | **Brief your manager** with the issue, what you have tried, and proposed options | Not the CSM who escalates everything, and not the one who escalates nothing |
| 5 | **Loop in an executive** when their executives are involved, or the resolution needs a business decision | Ask your executive about *their* timeline rather than assuming it matches yours |

Skipping to step 5 burns the escalation path and signals that the CSM cannot operate. Never
reaching step 5 loses accounts that one conversation would have held. The judgement is in
recognising which situation you are in, and the test below is how you tell.

## When to escalate

Escalate when **any** of these is true. All four are about authority, not about temperature.

| Trigger | Why an executive is required |
| --- | --- |
| The customer's own executives are engaged | Seniority asymmetry reads as indifference. Their VP should hear from our VP |
| The resolution needs a decision the CSM cannot make | A policy exception, a commercial structure, an engineering commitment, a roadmap decision |
| The economic buyer has left or changed role | The relationship must be re-established at the level it was lost (`R3`) |
| Severity is Critical | Notice served, intent to cancel, auto-renew off, or two P0 patterns |

Two supplementary triggers worth adopting `[P]`: any account in the top ARR decile that has been
red for two consecutive weeks goes to a weekly executive review, and **any stated intent to cancel
gets a same-day executive notification regardless of ARR** — because the notification costs
minutes and the alternative is an executive learning about it from the board pack.

## Choosing which executive

| Situation | Who | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Champion or economic buyer departed | VP CS or above | Positional weight, and it must not be the CSM asking who replaced their contact |
| Product gap, blocking requirement | VP CS plus a product leader | Only a product leader can commit or decline a date (`R19`) |
| Reliability failure with executive visibility | VP CS plus VP Engineering | The customer needs to hear from the person who owns the fix |
| Commercial structure beyond the approved band | CRO or CFO delegate | The decision is theirs; bringing them late wastes a cycle |
| M&A, consolidation mandate, new CIO/CFO | CCO or CEO | Peer level with the incoming decision-maker, who will not take a CSM meeting |
| Competitive re-bid | VP CS plus the AE's leader | The evaluation is scored on organisational commitment as much as product |

Match seniority to *their* attendee, not to the ARR. An executive who outranks everyone in the room
changes the dynamic in a way that rarely helps.

## The one-page brief

Written to be **forwarded unedited**. If it needs two pages, the situation is not yet understood.

| Field | Content specification |
| --- | --- |
| **Account · ARR · dates** | Name · $ARR · renews `<date>` · **opt-out `<date>` (`N` days)** |
| **Cause** | The root-cause code and its one-line definition. Not a symptom list |
| **What happened** | Three bullets. Facts with dates. No adjectives |
| **Evidence** | Two or three data points with provenance tags: a usage delta, ticket IDs, a verbatim quote |
| **Why now** | What forces the timeline — the notice window, their budget cycle, a competitor's evaluation date |
| **What we have tried** | A dated list with outcomes, including what did not work |
| **What we owe them** | Any commitment of ours that is overdue. This goes above the ask, not below it |
| **The ask** | **One specific thing**: a call, an engineering commitment, discount authority, a roadmap decision |
| **Decision needed by** | A date |
| **Owner** | One name |

**An escalation without a named ask and a decision date is a notification.** The discipline worth
importing from operations teams: send to-dos, not pings. If the executive reads the brief and does
not know what to do next, the brief failed, however accurate it was.

Two things that do **not** belong in the brief: a full account history, and your assessment of the
customer's people by name. The first wastes the page; the second is a firewall violation waiting
for a forward (`R18`).

## What the call must achieve

Exactly three outcomes. Anything else is a courtesy call, and courtesy calls consume the
escalation path without moving anything.

| # | Outcome | How you know it happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Their decision-maker states the problem in their own words** | You can quote them afterwards, and the quote is not what you assumed |
| 2 | **Our executive commits to something specific and dated** | It can be written as owner · action · date · success measure |
| 3 | **A next meeting is booked in the room** | It is on the calendar before the call ends, not "we'll find time" |

If outcome 1 does not happen, outcomes 2 and 3 are worthless — you have committed to solving a
problem you have not heard described.

## Running the call

| Phase | Minutes | Who leads | Content |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Frame | 2 | Our exec | Why they are here, what they can decide, and that they intend to listen first |
| Their account | 15 | Their decision-maker | Uninterrupted. Reflect back before responding at all |
| Surface everything else | 5 | Our exec | "What else is not working?" — repeat until they say nothing |
| Our position | 5 | Our exec | What we can commit, what we cannot, plainly and in that order |
| Decisions | 5 | Our exec | Owner, action, date for each |
| Next meeting | 3 | DRI | Booked on the call |

**Bad news first, once** (`R20`). If we broke something, our executive names it in the first two
minutes, quantifies the customer's cost, and says what changes. Leading with context reads as
defence, and a second apology asks the customer to absolve you, which makes your feelings their
job.

The CSM's role on the call is **not** to present. It is to have prepared the executive so
thoroughly that the executive can be present rather than briefed live.

## The six botch modes

| # | Botch | What it looks like | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Arriving with no decision to make** | The exec attends, listens, thanks everyone, and nothing changes. The customer concludes the escalation was theatre | One named ask and a decision-by date, agreed internally before the invite goes out |
| 2 | **The exec apologises instead of commits** | Warm, sincere, and empty. The customer has heard apologies; they wanted a date | Rehearse the specific commitment and its owner beforehand. One apology maximum, then act |
| 3 | **The exec commits something nobody owns** | A roadmap date, a fix window, a discount outside the band — said to relieve tension in the room | Pre-agree the commitment envelope. The DRI has explicit permission to say "let me confirm that and come back today" |
| 4 | **The exec is briefed in the corridor** | They open with a wrong fact, the customer corrects them, and the seniority becomes a liability | The one-page brief 24 hours ahead, plus a ten-minute pre-call |
| 5 | **Talking past the actual decision-maker** | The friendliest person in the room gets the attention while the person who decides watches | Identify the decision-maker in the brief and address them directly |
| 6 | **No follow-through** | Everything agreed, nothing written, and by week three both sides remember it differently | A written recap within 24 hours with owners and dates, from the DRI, not the exec |

A seventh, structural: **using the executive as the play**. Executive engagement is a step inside a
play, not a substitute for one. An exec call with no diagnosis behind it produces a commitment
aimed at the wrong cause.

## Exec-to-exec outreach

When the reason for escalation is a departed or unreachable buyer, the first move is a written
exec-to-exec note, not a meeting request. It works because it carries positional weight, asks for
nothing, and hands the agenda to them.

The pattern, in four sentences:

1. **Who you are and why you are writing** — one line, no title-dropping.
2. **The relationship's history in one fact** — when it started, what it produces, named.
3. **The honest reason for the note** — "with the change, I wanted you to hear from me rather than
   find us in a renewal notice."
4. **The ask, which is not a meeting** — "I do not need time. I need to know what you want this to
   deliver, so we can re-point it." Direct line included.

A second note from the CSM two to four days later carries the operational detail: the three things
in flight, the two decisions the new person now owns, and the offer of a one-page summary or twenty
minutes. Two notes, two altitudes, both from the live record rather than a template.

Send-ready versions are in `../assets/customer-comms.md`. Every line clears the firewall (`R18`):
no risk band, no ARR at risk, no inference stated as fact about the departure.

## After the call

| Within | Action | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same day | Internal debrief: what we heard that we did not expect, and what it changes in the diagnosis | DRI |
| 24 hours | Written recap to the customer with decisions, owners and dates — see `../assets/customer-comms.md` | DRI |
| 24 hours | Every commitment entered into the play's ledger with a success measure | DRI |
| 48 hours | Re-run the checkpoint test: did the call move a working signal? | DRI |
| Weekly | One-line update to the executive until closure, whether or not there is news | DRI |

The weekly line to the executive is what makes the *next* escalation possible. An executive who
hears nothing after the call learns that their time produced nothing they can see.

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Escalating before understanding the scope | Steps 1–4 first; an exec cannot escalate what you cannot describe |
| An exec brief that is an account history | Ten fields, one page, one ask, one date |
| "Can you join a call with Acme?" as the entire ask | Name the decision the exec is being asked to make |
| Matching seniority to ARR rather than to their attendee | Peer level with the person who decides |
| The CSM presenting for twenty minutes | The customer talks first and longest |
| An exec commitment with no owner | Nothing is committed in the room that is not internally agreed (`R19`) |
| No written recap | 24 hours, from the DRI, with owners and dates |
| Using the exec relationship as the save | The exec is a step in a play, not the play |
| Repeated escalations on the same account | Two in a year means the underlying cause was never fixed — re-diagnose |
| Sharing the risk band or ARR at risk in the room with the customer | Firewall (`R18`). The customer hears what we will do, never how we scored them |
