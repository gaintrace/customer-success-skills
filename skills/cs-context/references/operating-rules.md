# The Operating Rules

> Twenty-four rules this library holds as non-negotiable. Each has a name, a number, and the
> revenue consequence of breaking it.
>
> These are **opinions, held deliberately.** A skill that presents six options and no
> recommendation has moved the work back to the reader. Where the evidence supports a strong
> position, this library takes it, states the threshold, and says what would justify deviating.
> Where genuine uncertainty exists — probability without calibration, attribution without a
> holdout — it says so and refuses to fake a number. Those are different things.
>
> Cite rules by number. `R7` means something specific across every skill in this library.

## Protecting revenue you already have

### R1 · The Opt-Out Calendar
**Every renewal is scheduled against `renewal_date − notice_period_days`. Never the renewal date.**

A customer with 90 days' notice on a 1 February renewal decides in October. A team tracking
February is preparing for a date that has already passed. This is the single most common way a
renewal is lost for reasons unrelated to the product, and it is entirely preventable.

*Cost of breaking it:* renewals lost on a technicality, with a healthy account and a happy customer.
*Deviate when:* the contract is monthly evergreen — then there is no notice period and every day
is the deadline, which is a different discipline, not a relaxed one.

### R2 · Decisions Beat Indicators
**A commercial action sets a risk floor that no health score can override.**

Auto-renew switched off, notice served, termination language requested, a bulk data export by an
admin — these are not signals *about* a decision. They are the decision. A weighted score of 38
does not survive contact with an auto-renew flag that flipped on 2 August.

*Cost of breaking it:* the false-green churn. The account everyone was relaxed about.

### R3 · The 48-Hour Champion Rule
**Champion departure triggers exec-to-exec outreach within 48 hours, from a VP or above — not the CSM.**

Relationship equity concentrated in one person leaves with that person. The window in which a
successor is receptive is short, and a CSM asking "who has replaced Jamie?" signals that the
vendor was dependent on one relationship and has no other.

*Cost of breaking it:* the account enters the renewal with no internal advocate and no one who
remembers why it was bought.

### R4 · The Two-Pattern War Room
**Two or more P0 compound patterns on one account: stop scoring and open a war room the same day.**

Past two compound patterns the score has stopped being the useful artifact. Refining it is
displacement activity.

*Cost of breaking it:* a beautifully-scored account that churns while the analysis was being polished.

### R5 · The Single-Thread Tax
**An account with one engaged contact carries its full ARR as at-risk until a second relationship exists.**

Not a discount for being otherwise healthy. One person is one resignation, one reorg, one
maternity leave away from zero relationship. Enterprise target is six engaged contacts,
mid-market three, SMB two — engaged meaning a two-way interaction in 90 days, with persona
coverage, not a count of names in the CRM.

*Cost of breaking it:* R3 arrives and there is nothing underneath it.

### R6 · The Sponsor Rule
**No enterprise renewal enters Commit without an executive-sponsor meeting in the last two quarters.**

The person who signs is not the person who uses. A renewal forecast built on practitioner
enthusiasm is a forecast of the wrong person's opinion.

*Cost of breaking it:* forecast misses concentrated in the largest accounts, which is the
category of miss that costs a VP their credibility with the CFO.

### R7 · Paper Starts at T-90
**Security review, MSA redlines, vendor onboarding and PO issuance begin 90 days before the
opt-out deadline — earlier in regulated verticals.**

These are calendar dependencies, not formalities. A signed-in-principle renewal that misses its
own notice window because procurement took seven weeks is still a churn on the board slide.

*Cost of breaking it:* slipped renewals that were never at risk commercially.

## Finding revenue inside the base

### R8 · The Health Gate
**No expansion ask on an account below the Watch band. State that you withheld it, and why.**

Selling into an unhealthy account converts a recoverable risk into a churn, because it tells the
customer you were not paying attention to the thing they were struggling with. The one exception
is a genuinely separate business unit with its own budget, its own users and its own health.

*Cost of breaking it:* you lose the renewal and the expansion, and the CSM relationship with it.

### R9 · The 3× Value Rule
**Do not ask for expansion until demonstrated, quantified value is at least three times the increment.**

A customer asked for £40k more needs to already believe they received £120k. Below that ratio the
conversation becomes a negotiation about price rather than a decision about capacity.

*Cost of breaking it:* the ask is refused, and the refusal anchors the renewal conversation low.

### R10 · The Constraint Rule
**Only sell against a constraint the customer can feel. No felt constraint, no opportunity — however strong the signal.**

96% seat utilisation is a fact. "Three people asked me for access last week and I said no" is a
constraint. The first is a reason to look; only the second is a reason to ask.

*Cost of breaking it:* a pipeline of "opportunities" nobody works and nobody forecasts, which
destroys the credibility of every real one.

### R11 · Value First, Ask Second — Never Both
**Never make a commercial ask in a conversation where you delivered bad news, an apology, or a miss.**

Separate the meetings. An ask attached to an apology reads as leverage, and customers remember it
for years.

*Cost of breaking it:* permanent loss of the good-faith assumption, which is worth more than the ask.

### R12 · The Co-Term Rule
**Expansions inside 90 days of the opt-out deadline co-term with the renewal. Outside 90 days, run separately.**

Inside the window a separate paper cycle competes with the renewal for the same procurement
capacity. Outside it, bundling hands the customer one large number to negotiate against instead
of two small ones to approve.

*Cost of breaking it:* an expansion that delays a renewal, which is the worst possible trade.

## Spending attention well

### R13 · The Capacity Truth
**Plan against usable hours, not nominal hours. Usable is roughly 60% of a week.**

Meetings, admin, reactive interrupts, internal escalations and recruiting consume the rest. A
book plan built on 40 hours produces a queue nobody finishes and a CSM who stops trusting the plan.

*Cost of breaking it:* every account gets a little attention and none gets enough.

### R14 · The Written Skip
**An account you decide not to work this cycle is written down, with a reason and a revisit date.**

Capacity forces triage; that is legitimate. What is not legitimate is an unwritten decision,
because it is indistinguishable from an oversight and it repeats silently for four quarters.

*Cost of breaking it:* accounts that rot without anyone having decided to let them.

### R15 · The QBR Kill Rule
**No decision to make means no QBR. Send the one-pager and give the hour back.**

A QBR held because the calendar said so trains the customer that these meetings are ceremonial,
and the one that matters gets declined along with the rest.

*Cost of breaking it:* declining attendance at exactly the meeting you need when the account is
in trouble.

### R16 · The Three-Question Limit
**Three discovery questions per call. Ask one, then stop talking.**

The second half of the answer arrives after the silence. A list of ten questions gets four
shallow answers and no discovery.

*Cost of breaking it:* an interrogation the customer endures rather than a conversation they
learn something from.

### R17 · One Play Per Account
**Run one primary play. Stacking three produces three half-executed interventions.**

The pattern selects the play. An account receiving simultaneous adoption outreach, an exec
escalation and an expansion pitch experiences a vendor in disarray.

*Cost of breaking it:* effort without effect, and a customer who feels managed rather than helped.

## Not destroying trust

### R18 · The Firewall
**Internal assessment never crosses to the customer. Not softened, not hinted at, not translated.**

Health score, risk band, ARR at risk, forecast category, save play, any judgement of a named
person. A leaked risk score ends renewals that were not otherwise at risk, because the customer
learns two things at once: that you were grading them, and that you never said so.

*Cost of breaking it:* the relationship, immediately and permanently.

### R19 · No Date You Do Not Own
**Never commit a roadmap date, a fix date, or a delivery date without the named owner having agreed it.**

"It's on the roadmap" is the kindest-sounding sentence in customer success and the most
damaging. A clear no, with reasoning and the nearest alternative, preserves more trust than a
soft yes that turns out to be false.

*Cost of breaking it:* the second missed date, after which nothing you say is believed.

### R20 · Bad News First, Once
**Open with the miss. Apologise once, then act.**

A customer who has had a bad quarter knows it. Leading with good news tells them you either did
not notice or hoped they had not. And a second apology asks them to absolve you, which makes
your feelings their job.

*Cost of breaking it:* the credibility gap, which costs more than the original failure.

### R21 · The Stop-Loss
**Every save has a spend ceiling and an exit date. When it is reached, exit gracefully.**

A save pursued past the point of return consumes the hours that would have protected three
healthy accounts, and it ends badly anyway. A managed exit preserves the win-back and the
reference; a fought exit destroys both.

*Cost of breaking it:* the account leaves anyway, angrier, and three others were neglected.

## Telling the truth about numbers

### R22 · Ordering Before Probability
**Without a backtest you have a ranking, not a forecast. Bands only.**

A rules-based score expressed as "87% likely to churn" is a fabricated number with a decimal
point on it. Ordering is what a CSM acts on and needs far less evidence.
See `calibration-loop.md`.

*Cost of breaking it:* the first miss discredits the entire model, permanently.

### R23 · The Coverage Cap
**Confidence never exceeds what coverage permits.** A 95%-confident read of 40% of the picture is
a 40% read. Print all seven signal families, including the missing ones, and name what the gaps
typically hide.

*Cost of breaking it:* confident analysis of the half of the account you could see.

### R24 · Label the Decision, Not the Event
**Churn is dated when the customer decided, not when the contract ended.**

A December churn decided in September is a September event. Get this wrong and any model you
build has learned your notice period and nothing else.

*Cost of breaking it:* a predictive model that predicts the calendar.

## When to break a rule

These are strong defaults, not physics. Break one when you can state all three:

1. **Which rule**, by number.
2. **The specific circumstance** that makes it wrong here — not "this account is different".
3. **What you will watch** to know whether breaking it was right.

Write those three lines into the artifact. A rule broken on the record is a judgement call. A
rule broken silently is the thing the rule existed to prevent.

The rules that should almost never be broken, because the downside is unrecoverable rather than
merely expensive: **R18** (the firewall), **R19** (dates you do not own), **R22** (probability
without a backtest).
