# Delivering Bad News Upward

> The structure that keeps credibility when the quarter did not work. Missing a number does
> not end a career. Being the last person in the room to say it out loud does.

## 1. The governing principle

**Never surprise an executive in the room.** Bad news travels on a schedule you choose or a
schedule someone else chooses. The single most reliable predictor of whether a miss damages
your standing is whether the CEO, the CFO and your lead director heard it from you first,
privately, with a plan already forming.

The corollary matters as much: **share bad news regularly, not only when it is unavoidable.**
A leader whose monthly update contains one honest amber every month is believed when they
report a green. A leader whose updates are uniformly green until a crisis has trained the room
to discount everything they say. Regular, mixed reporting is what makes a single bad quarter
survivable — it normalises the act of reporting badly.

Three failure modes account for almost all the damage:

| Failure | What it looks like | Why it costs more than the miss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Late** | The board learns from the CFO's model, an investor's portfolio review, or a departing customer | You are no longer the source of truth about your own function |
| **Soft** | "Some headwinds in the SMB segment" | Vagueness reads as either not knowing or not telling. Both are worse than the number |
| **Serial re-forecasting** | The number moves in a different direction at every meeting | Repeated revision destroys more trust than one large miss, because it removes the ability to plan |

## 2. The six-part structure

In this order, in the first 60 seconds of the slide. Do not reorder it — every reordering is a
recognisable evasion.

| # | Element | Rule | Bad | Good |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Name it first** | Plain language, first person, no hedge, no preamble | "We saw some pressure on gross retention this quarter" | "We missed GRR by 110 basis points. That is $1.1M against plan." |
| 2 | **Quantify precisely** | Dollars, account count, segment, tenure. Precision *is* the apology | "A number of SMB accounts churned" | "$3.1M SMB churn across 90 logos, median tenure 11 months, average size 1.67× the SMB book average." |
| 3 | **Separate what you knew from what you did not** | The line that buys the room. State the detection gap as a number | "We were tracking most of these" | "We flagged $7.4M of the $13.1M loss 60+ days out. $5.7M was a surprise. The 43.5% surprise rate is the real finding, not the churn." |
| 4 | **Root cause in one sentence** | No architecture lecture, no chain of contributing factors. Detail goes in the appendix | "A combination of factors including macro conditions and…" | "SMB accounts that miss go-live by day 30 churn at 2.4× the rate of those that don't, and 61% missed it." |
| 5 | **State the change** | One systemic fix — what prevents the *class* of failure. Owner, date, cost | "We're going to focus on onboarding" | "SMB onboarding is gated on a 30-day activation milestone from 1 October. Owner: VP Onboarding. Cost: two FTEs redeployed from Mid-Market." |
| 6 | **State the leading indicator you will report next time** | Name the metric and the date, unprompted — then report it whether or not it worked | "We'll keep you posted" | "Next board I open with SMB 30-day activation rate and M3 dollar retention for the September and October cohorts." |

Then **stop**. One clear pass, owned, then the plan. Dwelling on the negative is itself a
credibility problem: it reads as either self-flagellation or an attempt to exhaust the topic
before the questions start.

**A pack with no amber is itself bad news.** A uniformly green scorecard tells an experienced
director that you are either not measuring or not telling. Carry at least one honest amber
every period.

## 3. The pre-brief sequence

| When | Who | What you say | What you want back |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **T−7 days** | CFO | The number, the driver, the reconciliation. Bring the arithmetic, not the narrative | Agreement that the number is the number. Never debate the figure in the board meeting |
| **T−5 days** | CEO | The number, the driver, the response, and the ask. Say explicitly: "this is the worst slide in the pack" | Their view on the ask, and whether they want to carry any part of it |
| **T−3 days** | Lead director / board chair (by phone, not email) | The headline and the plan, in three minutes | Whether the board will want a deeper session; better to know now |
| **T−3 days** | The pre-read goes out | The full five-sentence spine including the miss | Nothing. It is delivery, not consultation |
| **T−1 day** | Your own team | What you are going to say, so nobody hears it secondhand | Corrections to fact, not to framing |

**Call, do not email, for a material miss.** Email and text are for logistics; a serious number
gets a voice, because a voice can answer the follow-up in the same minute. The pre-read is
sent afterwards, as confirmation of a conversation that already happened.

## 4. Separating what you knew from what you did not

This is the part almost every leader skips, and it is the part that determines whether the
board concludes "bad quarter" or "bad leader". A board can tolerate loss. It cannot tolerate
not knowing whether you can see loss coming.

Report three buckets, in dollars, always:

| Bucket | Definition | What it says about you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Flagged and lost** | At-risk state entered ≥60 days before the loss, with a dated flag | Your detection works; the intervention did not. That is a playbook problem — solvable |
| **Flagged late** | Flagged inside 60 days | You saw it after the decision was made. That is a signal-lead-time problem — solvable with instrumentation |
| **Never flagged** | No at-risk state before the loss | You cannot see this class of risk at all. That is the honest finding, and the strongest basis for an ask |

The composite metric is the **risk detection rate**: `ARR flagged ≥60d before loss / total ARR
lost`. Below roughly 60%, most of your churn is a surprise and your renewal forecast is
fiction [P]. Publishing a poor detection rate voluntarily is one of the highest-return moves
available to a CS leader — it converts a performance conversation into an instrumentation
conversation, and instrumentation gets funded.

**The flag date must be immutable.** If flags can be backdated, the metric is theatre and an
auditor or a sharp director will find it. Freeze the snapshot.

## 5. The language table

| Never say | Say instead | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Headwinds" | "$1.1M below plan" | Weather metaphors are how people avoid subtraction |
| "Challenges around retention" | "GRR 86.9% against plan of 88.0%" | Name the metric and the gap |
| "It wasn't the right fit" | "We sold into an ICP we cannot retain — 61% of these logos came from paid search and churn at 2.1× the outbound cohort" | Excusing a loss reads as not having diagnosed it |
| "We're monitoring the situation" | "The next checkpoint is 15 October; if 30-day activation is under 60% we stop taking that segment inbound" | An unfalsifiable commitment is not a commitment |
| "We'll drive adoption" | "Two onboarding FTEs, gated go-live, measured by 30-day activation rate" | A verb is not a plan |
| "Directionally we think…" | The number, or `UNKNOWN — requires <source>` | Directional language is where fabrications live |
| "Unprecedented" | "This is the second consecutive quarter" | Boards remember |
| "To be fair to the team…" | Nothing. Defend people privately, own results publicly | Defending in the room reads as deflecting |
| "We'll get back to you on that" | "You will have it by Thursday" | A date converts an evasion into a commitment |

## 6. Four worked examples

### 6.1 A GRR miss concentrated in one segment

> "We missed gross retention by 110 basis points — 86.9% against a plan of 88.0%, which is
> $1.1M. Ninety-four percent of the miss is SMB: SMB GRR came in at 76.9% against a plan of
> 84%. Of $3.1M in SMB churn, $1.9M is reason-coded *never reached go-live* — those accounts
> had a median time-to-value of 71 days against a 30-day target. We flagged $1.2M of that
> $1.9M more than 60 days out and saved none of it, which tells me the problem is upstream of
> the save motion, not inside it. From 1 October, SMB onboarding is gated on a 30-day
> activation milestone, and two onboarding FTEs move from Mid-Market. Owner is our VP of
> Onboarding. Next board I will open with SMB 30-day activation rate and M3 dollar retention
> for the September and October cohorts. Enterprise and Mid-Market are on plan."

### 6.2 A single large logo lost

> "We lost <Account>, $620k, at renewal on 12 August. That is 62 basis points of GRR on its
> own and it takes Enterprise GRR from 91.2% to 90.4%. The economic buyer left in March; we
> did not identify a replacement for 71 days, and by the time we did, procurement had already
> run a competitive process. We knew in April — it was flagged at-risk on 9 April, 125 days
> out — so this is not a detection failure, it is a save failure, and specifically an
> executive-coverage failure: we had no exec sponsor mapped to the account. From this quarter
> every account above $250k has a named executive sponsor with a quarterly contact
> requirement, and the coverage gap is reported on this slide each quarter. Twelve of our
> top-20 accounts currently have no sponsor; closing that is the ask on slide 10."

### 6.3 A forecast that was wrong

> "Last quarter I called $19.8M and we closed $18.9M — 95.5% accuracy, but biased 4.8%
> optimistic, and that is the number that matters. It is the third consecutive quarter of
> positive bias. The pattern is in Commit: 11% of Commit ARR closed lost or downsold, against
> a 5% tolerance. Every one of those accounts had no economic-buyer contact inside 60 days at
> the time of the call. Commit now requires a logged economic-buyer interaction within 45
> days; applying that gate retroactively would have moved $1.4M out of last quarter's Commit
> and produced a 0.6% bias instead of 4.8%. This quarter's call already excludes that $1.4M
> equivalent, which is why the number looks lower than the trend would suggest."

### 6.4 A definition change

> "One number on slide 3 is restated. We were including usage overage in the ARR base;
> from this quarter we exclude it, consistent with the SaaS Metrics Standard Board treatment,
> because overage volatility was making single-period NRR unreadable. The effect is to lower
> reported NRR by 210 basis points across all eight periods shown. Both series are on the
> slide — as-reported in grey, restated in black — and the change log entry is A2. Nothing
> about customer behaviour changed; the measurement did. I would rather take the 210 points
> once than have you find it later."

## 7. Credibility destroyers

| Destroyer | Why it is fatal |
| --- | --- |
| Going dark between meetings | Silence is read as concealment, and the read is usually correct |
| Letting the board hear it from a third party | You are no longer the source of truth about your own function |
| Email or text for a material miss | It removes the follow-up question from the same conversation and leaves the reader to imagine the worst |
| Downplaying with "wasn't the right fit" | Reads as an undiagnosed loss |
| Changing the forecast direction at every meeting | Removes the ability to plan; costs more trust than the miss |
| Inflated confidence categories with no evidence | One quarter of Commit leakage and every future call is discounted |
| Blaming the team, the market, or another function | Boards fund people who own outcomes and defend people privately |
| Trading bad news for a discount reflexively | Concessions offered in response to a miss become the expected response to every future miss |
| Bad news in the appendix | Its position is itself a statement, and directors read positions |
| No amber, ever | Tells the room you are not measuring or not telling |

## 8. Calibrating candour by audience

| Audience | How much | Specific rules |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Board** | Everything material, in dollars, with the detection split | Pre-brief the CEO, CFO and lead director. No account names beyond the top-10 renewal status |
| **Exec staff** | Everything, plus the internal disagreement about the cause | This is the only room where you say "my team and I do not agree on the driver, and here is the test that settles it" |
| **Investor update** | Everything material, **in writing, early** | Bad news in writing before the meeting is what makes the meeting short. Interleave with the good so the pattern is normal |
| **All-hands** | Honest, directional, no per-account dollars | No at-risk names, no ARR-at-risk totals, no save lists. Assume a screenshot. Say what changed and what the team is being asked to do |
| **The customer** | Nothing from this pack | Internal risk language must never appear in customer-facing material. See `../../cs-context/references/evidence-standard.md` §9 |

## 9. After the meeting

| Action | Timing | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Send the written summary of what you committed to, with owners and dates | Same day | The commitment is socially freshest immediately; a week later it is negotiable |
| Put the leading indicator you named into next period's pack — first | Next cycle | Reporting your own metric unprompted, including when it did not move, is the single most efficient way to rebuild a forecast's credibility |
| Report the intervention's result whether or not it worked | Next cycle | A leader who reports their own failed intervention gets the next ask approved |
| Log the miss in the change log if any definition moved | Immediately | Silent restatement later is far more expensive than disclosure now |

**Sources.** Founder Collective, *How to Break Bad News to Investors* (Micah Rosenbloom, 2018)
— pre-brief individually, call rather than email, avoid serial re-forecasting [V]. The
own-it structure is this library's own `R20 · Bad News First, Once`
(`../../cs-context/references/operating-rules.md`) — one clear owned pass, then act.
Risk detection rate and its ~60% threshold are a practitioner rule of thumb
[P]. Forecast bias tolerances (|bias| ≤2%, Commit leakage ≤5%) are practitioner targets [P].
