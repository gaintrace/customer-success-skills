# Saying No

> The most valuable sentence in the forward-deployed role and the least practised. This file
> covers the three things that go wrong around it: **writing the decline** so it holds and the
> relationship survives, **escalating to product** without handing over a date you do not own, and
> **pricing the trade** when a signature depends on the answer.
>
> A no is not the absence of a decision. It is a decision, and it carries the same obligations as
> a yes: a reason in the customer's interest, the nearest alternative priced, a written record and
> a revisit date (`R14`).
>
> Every block below is send-ready as written. Nothing here may carry a carrying cost, an interest
> rate, the outcome word, margin, PS utilisation, renewal exposure, or a comparison to another
> customer (`R18`). Run the leak scan in `../../cs-context/references/customer-voice.md` over every
> draft before it goes.
>
> Evidence labels: `[M]` measured · `[V]` vendor research · `[P]` practitioner standard ·
> `[A]` academic, standards body or regulation · `[D]` primary document.

## 1. Why the soft yes is the expensive answer

Three sentences do more damage in this role than any decline ever has: **"it's on the roadmap"**,
**"we'll take a look at it"**, and **"we'll productise it later"**. Each buys quiet in the meeting
and spends credibility that the next conversation will need. The second missed date is the one
after which nothing you say is believed (`R19`).

The customer's experience of a soft yes is not relief, it is **effort** — repeated follow-ups,
chased dates, a question that never closes. CEB's customer-effort research found **96% of customers
with a high-effort service interaction became more disloyal, against 9% after a low-effort one**
`[V · Dixon, Freeman & Toman, CEB, 2010; *The Effortless Experience*, 2013]`. A clean no closes the
loop in one exchange. A soft yes opens a thread that costs them attention every month until
somebody admits it was never happening.

William Ury's structure is the one that works, and it is worth naming because the middle step is
the one people drop: **Yes — No — Yes?** Root the refusal in what you are protecting (their
upgrade path, their data, the thing they actually bought), state the limit plainly, then propose
the next door `[P · Ury, *The Power of a Positive No*, 2007]`. A no with no third step is a
rejection; a no with no second step is a soft yes wearing a decline's clothes.

**The no belongs in its own moment.** Never attach it to an apology, a miss, or a commercial ask
(`R11`) — a refusal delivered alongside a request reads as leverage, and customers remember that
for years. And if the decline is genuinely bad news for them, it follows a call rather than
arriving as the first notification (`C26`).

## 2. The anatomy of a decline

Four parts, in this order. The order is the mechanism: a decision buried under context reads as
negotiable, and every extra sentence before the verdict invites a counter-proposal.

| # | Part | Requirement | The failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **The decision, first** | One sentence, active voice, in the first line: "We're not going to build X." | Opening with context, which reads as defence and makes the reader hunt for the answer |
| 2 | **The reason, in their interest** | What it would cost *them* — a path that breaks silently, a dependency they carry, an upgrade they cannot take | An internal reason: "it's not on our roadmap", "we don't have capacity". Both are true and neither is theirs |
| 3 | **The nearest alternative, priced** | Specific, with what it covers, what it does not, what it costs them in time, and a named person to run it | "There are some workarounds" — a category, not an offer |
| 4 | **What happens next, with a date you own** | The field-signal request, and the date you will tell them the decision — never the decision itself | A roadmap date (`R19`), or silence, which the customer reads as the request being lost |

**Say the residual gap out loud.** A decline that describes only what the alternative covers leaves
the customer to discover the remainder on their own, at the worst moment. Naming it — "what's left
is the supplier-code translation, about twenty minutes of Priya's morning" — is what makes the
rest of the message credible.

**Then stop talking** (`C3`). The instinct after a refusal is to soften it, and every softening
sentence negotiates against yourself. The silence does the work.

### The reference block

Worked in full, no slots left open. Swap: account, contact, the job, the alternative, the residual,
the person, the decision date.

```text
Subject: The nightly close-out export — what we can do, and what we won't

Hi Dana,

We're not going to build the close-out export as a custom job for Meridian,
and you should have the real reason rather than a soft no: it would sit
outside the upgrade path, and the first time either side changed a field it
would fail quietly, most likely at month-end.

Here's what covers the part that costs you time. The scheduled report already
produces the same eleven fields, and I can have it land in your SFTP drop at
05:45 instead of the UI, which removes the manual download your team does at
06:00. What's left is the supplier-code translation, about twenty minutes of
Priya's morning; I've written the mapping as a formula so it's two minutes,
and I'll sit with her on Thursday to hand it over.

I've written the translation gap up for our product team, in your words. I'm
not giving you a date, because I don't own one — I'll tell you what they
decide, either way, by 30 September.

Does Thursday at 10 work for the handover with Priya?

Thanks,
Jo
```

More variants — work-around, deferral, half-built pilot, retirement, escalation reply:
`../assets/decline-note.md`.

## 3. Sentence level

| Instead of | Write | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Unfortunately we're unable to accommodate that request at this time." | "We're not going to build that." | Passive and hedged reads as a decision somebody else made. Own it |
| "It's on the roadmap." | "I don't own a date for that, so I'm not going to give you one. What I can tell you is when we'll decide: 30 September." | `R19`. The kindest-sounding sentence in customer success and the most damaging |
| "We'll look into it." | "I've written it up for our product team with your words in it, and I'll come back on 30 September either way." | An action with an owner and a date, instead of a direction of travel |
| "That's not currently supported." | "The supported path gets you the same eleven fields at 05:45; what it won't do is translate the supplier codes." | States what they get and what they do not, in one sentence |
| "We could potentially explore a custom option." | "A custom build here would sit outside the upgrade path, so we're not doing it." | A door left ajar is a door the customer will keep pushing |
| "Our engineering team is at capacity." | "This one would break the first time either side changed a field — that risk sits with you, and I'm not comfortable creating it." | Our constraint is not their problem. Their exposure is |
| "I'll escalate this internally." | "I've sent it to Ana Ruiz, who owns integrations, with the three accounts that hit the same problem. She decides by 15 October." | `R18` bars the internal machinery; a named person and a date is what they wanted from the word |
| "We really value your partnership and hope you understand." | "You pushed us on the audit-log export last year and you were right. This one I'm answering differently, and here's why." | An assertion of the relationship replaced by evidence of it |
| "Sorry again for the disappointing news." | *(delete — one apology, at most, and only if we caused the situation)* | A second apology asks the customer to absolve you (`C28`, `R20`) |

**Register.** No exclamation marks, no superlatives, no intensifiers, sentences under 25 words
(`C27`). Where the customer is angry, reflect their own words back accurately before answering the
content at all (`C4`) — arguing the facts first raises the temperature, naming the state lowers it.

**Never in a decline:** the word "bespoke", the outcome word, the carrying cost, the interest rate,
the ARR at stake, PS margin, another customer named or identifiable, our internal prioritisation,
or any variation of "we prioritised another account" (`R18`).

## 4. The revenue-pressured trade

When a signature or a renewal depends on the build, the pressure is real and absorbing it silently
is the worst available answer. **An absorbed build is a discount that never appears in the discount
report**, and unlike a discount it recurs every year the component lives and nobody reprices it.

```
annual_trade_price     = TCO(N) ÷ term_years
effective_discount_pct = TCO(N) ÷ (arr × term_years) × 100
```

Kestrel Health, worked (`carrying-cost.md` §4.3): `TCO(3) = $460k` against $600k ARR on a
three-year term → **$150k a year, an effective discount of 25.5% over the term.** No commercial
leader would approve a 25.5% discount in a sentence; several have approved this build in one.

### Three structures — default to B

| | Structure | Use when | What it costs them | What we get |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | **Funded as services at cost** | Generality is low and the customer holds budget | `TCO(N)` as a fee, with the maintenance line quoted **separately** from the build line | Margin, and a customer who now prices the thing they are asking for |
| B | **Built for a term commitment and a sunset clause (default)** | ARR at stake exceeds `TCO(N)` and the job is durable | A multi-year term, a named maintenance window, and written agreement that the component sunsets when the supported path ships | Term, a sunset that is theirs as well as ours, and a renewal that is not re-opened annually |
| C | **Declined, with a discount sized to the gap** | The workaround reaches most of the job and the residual is worth less than `annual_trade_price` | A discount that is visible, reported, and repriceable at renewal | A number in the discount report where it belongs, and no component in the register |

**Quote maintenance as its own line, always.** A single build number teaches the customer that the
thing is finished when it ships, and the renewal conversation two years later starts from that
belief. Two lines — build, then annual maintenance — is the entire difference between a fee and an
argument.

**Never concede without trading** (`C12`). Every structure above has a "what we get" column and it
cannot be empty. Test authority before you concede anything (`C14`): *"if I can get that approved,
is this something you're able to sign this quarter?"* A concession given to someone without signing
authority is spent twice.

**Announce the structure, do not ask for it** (`C13`). "Here's how we'd do it: a three-year term
and a sunset clause" lands. "Would you be open to a longer term?" invites a counter before the
conversation has started.

### The trade, in their words

Structure B, sent after the call it was discussed on. Swap: account, contact, the component, the
term, the maintenance window, the sunset condition, the date.

```text
Subject: The approval-matrix work — how we'd make it work

Hi Marcus,

Following our call: we can build the approval matrix for Kestrel, and I want
to be straight about the shape it has to take, because a version of this that
nobody maintains is worse for you than not having it.

Three things travel together:

  • We build it, and it goes live in your January release window.
  • Sam Okafor owns it by name, with a maintenance window every second
    Tuesday. If it breaks, he is the person paged, not a queue.
  • It runs on a three-year term, and it retires when the standard approval
    engine covers your rules — we'll show you that comparison before any
    switch, and the switch is your call, not ours.

The third point is the one I'd push back on if I were you, so here is the
reasoning: a component built for one customer stops getting better the day it
ships. Tying it to the standard path is how you avoid being the only company
running a version nobody else has.

If that shape works, I'll have the amendment to you on Thursday and we can
keep the January window. If it doesn't, tell me which part and I'll come back
with an alternative rather than a softer version of the same thing.

Thanks,
Jo
```

**Structure C, said plainly:** *"We're not building it. What I can do is take 6% off the renewal,
which is roughly what the manual step costs your team in a year. That's a real number and it goes
in the contract, rather than a build that would cost you more in the long run and cost you your
upgrade path."* Say the number and stop (`C3`).

## 5. Escalating to product

The escalation is a document, not a volume control. Send the §5 request from
`productisation-path.md` — the job, the customers' words, `N_evidenced`, the search with its
negatives, the carrying cost, the general shape, the ARR unblocked, and the decision date you need.

| Do | Do not |
| --- | --- |
| Ask for a **decision by a date** | Ask for a roadmap slot, which no PM can grant and every PM will defer |
| Name the earliest opt-out deadline in the evidence table as the constraint | Use the renewal date — the customer decides `notice_period_days + evidence_window` earlier (`R1`) |
| Escalate once, on new evidence: a new account at G3+, a written renewal condition, an ARR change | Re-raise the same evidence louder. It spends credibility the next request needs |
| Write the fallback and its carrying cost into the request before sending | Leave the fallback unwritten, which turns a decline into a second discovery exercise |
| Accept "no, because X" as a complete answer and act on it that week | Treat a decline as a starting position to be negotiated |

**The internal machinery never crosses the divider** (`R18`). The customer hears: *"I've written it
up for our product team, in your words, and I'll tell you what they decide by 30 September."* They
do not hear who owns it internally, how it was prioritised, how many other accounts asked, or what
we would build if they escalated.

**Chase once, in writing, on the date.** If the decision date passes without an answer, the
component's disposition is decided without it: price the fallback, update the register, tell the
customer what you told them you would tell them. **Silence on the day you promised is the single
fastest way to convert a well-handled decline into a trust problem.**

## 6. The six pushbacks

Each of these arrives after a decline, and each has one correct answer. Reflect the words back
first (`C4`), then answer the content.

| Pushback | What it usually means | The answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **"Your competitor would build this."** | Testing whether the no is real | "They might. What I can tell you is what happens after: a version only you run, that we can't upgrade, on a support path that isn't the one you bought." Never name a competitor they did not raise, never disparage |
| **"We're one of your largest customers."** | True, and being used as leverage | "You are, and that's exactly why I'm not putting a component into your stack that only you run. What I'll do for an account this size is give you a named owner and a decision date on the underlying gap." Size justifies owning something explicitly; it never justifies leaving the upgrade path |
| **"The renewal depends on this."** | Sometimes true, usually a strong preference | Establish whether it is written. If it is, go to §4 and price the trade. If it is not, score rubric dimension 8 as 0 until it is, and keep the two conversations separate (`R11`) |
| **"Just do it as a quick script, it doesn't need to be supported."** | They are trying to help | "An unsupported script is a supported thing the day your month-end depends on it." The fourth workaround shape — us performing a manual step — is a build wearing different clothes; price it as one (`decision-rubric.md` §2) |
| **"Can you at least give us a rough timeframe?"** | Reasonable, and the trap | "I don't own that date, so any number I give you is a guess with my name on it. Here's the date I do own: 30 September, when I'll tell you what was decided." (`R19`) |
| **"We'll escalate to your VP."** | Fine — help them | "That's reasonable. Priya Raman is the right person and I'll send her the same document I sent product, so you're not starting from scratch." Then send it, before they do (§7) |

**The real competitor is no-decision** (`C16`), and the second-order risk of a decline is not that
they leave for a rival — it is that the request sits unresolved until the renewal, where it becomes
the reason nobody can articulate a business case. That is why part 4 of the anatomy is not optional.

## 7. When it goes over your head

| Step | Action | Timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Brief your own escalation point **before** the customer reaches them, with the decision, the gate that returned it, the numbers and the alternative offered | Same day you learn of it |
| 2 | Give them the customer's strongest argument in the customer's own words, not your rebuttal of it | With the brief |
| 3 | State what you would need to change your answer — the specific evidence, not a threshold of pressure | With the brief |
| 4 | Let the decision be re-made if the numbers changed; a decline overturned on new evidence is the system working | At the review |
| 5 | If it is overturned on seniority alone, record it as a deviation: the rule number, the circumstance, and what will be watched | Same week |

**A decision reversed without new evidence is not a reversal, it is an exception, and it must be
written as one.** Otherwise the gate sequence is decorative and every future request is a
negotiation about who is in the room. **Gate 0 is the one that is never overturned:** a fork has no
upgrade path and no support contract, and no revenue, score or seniority changes that.

## 8. The written record

A decline that lives only in a conversation repeats next quarter with nobody able to say what
changed. **A decline is a written record with a reason and a revisit date** (`R14`).

| Field | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Request, job, trigger | The customer's words, quoted and dated, with the source |
| Outcome and gate | The word, and the gate that returned it |
| Numbers | `TCO(N)`, interest rate, share of ARR, `N_evidenced`, `K*` — internal only |
| What was offered instead | The alternative, its fidelity against the job, its cost to them |
| What we told the customer | The date the note was sent, and the date we owe them an answer |
| Field signal | Link to the §5 request and the product counterpart's decision date |
| Revisit date | The next annual cull or the next renewal, whichever is sooner |
| Falsifier | The observable events that would flip a named gate |

**The revisit date is the difference between a decision and an ending.** Set it explicitly, put it
in the register row, and make the account's next cull re-run the gates with that day's ARR — the
component that failed Gate 4 at $150k of ARR may pass at $600k, and the request that scored
`N_evidenced = 1.0` may have three G3 accounts behind it by then.

## 9. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Softening a decline with "it's on the roadmap" | `R19`. No date without a named owner who agreed it. Give the decision date instead |
| Burying the decision under three paragraphs of context | Decision first, reason second, alternative third. Context that precedes the verdict reads as defence |
| A reason expressed in our terms ("we don't have capacity") | State what it would cost **them**: a silent failure at month-end, an upgrade they cannot take |
| Offering the alternative as a category | "Some workarounds exist" is not an offer. Name it, price it, say what it does not cover, give it a person and a date |
| Absorbing the build to save a deal | State `effective_discount_pct`, recommend one structure, quote maintenance as its own line. An invisible discount recurs annually |
| Conceding without trading | `C12`. Every structure carries what we get: term, reference, volume, payment terms, a sunset clause |
| Attaching the no to an apology or an ask | `R11`. Separate moments. A refusal beside a request reads as leverage |
| A second apology | `C28`, `R20`. Once, then act. A second apology makes your feelings their job |
| Escalating the same evidence louder | Escalate once, on new evidence, in writing. Volume spends the credibility the next request needs |
| A decline with no revisit date | `R14`. A decline without a date is an ending, and the same request returns next quarter unchanged |
| Naming a competitor, another customer, or the internal prioritisation | `R18`. The reply says what we will do and what we will not; it never explains our queue |
