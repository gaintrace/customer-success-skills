# The Objection Bank

> Prepared answers for the questions that decide meetings. Every entry gives the **surface
> question**, the **real question underneath it**, the **answer structure**, the **evidence to
> have ready**, and **what to do if pressed**.
>
> Two rules govern all of them. First: never answer an objection you have not confirmed —
> "help me understand what's driving that" costs one sentence and prevents answering the wrong
> question. Second: the evidence must be to hand before the call, not promised during it.
> "Let me get back to you" on a price objection in a renewal conversation is a lost quarter.

## Price and budget

### "It's too expensive."

**Underneath:** almost never about the absolute number. It is one of — the value is not
visible, the budget genuinely shrank, they have a cheaper quote, or they are testing.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Answer structure** | Confirm which one it is *before* answering. "Is this about the number itself, about what it's delivering, or about the budget you've been given this year?" Each has a different answer and a discount is the right answer to only one of them. |
| **Evidence ready** | Value delivered with the arithmetic; cost per active user; what the increment buys; the alternative structures (term, tier, payment terms) |
| **If pressed** | Never concede price on the first ask. Trade: a longer term, a case study, a reference, a multi-year commitment, earlier payment. Every concession buys something — see `renewal-negotiation` §concession ladder. |
| **Never say** | "I'll see what I can do" — it concedes the principle before you know the problem |

### "We need to cut costs across the board."

**Underneath:** an exogenous constraint, not a judgement on you. Adoption arguments will land badly.

Lead with "how do we make this survivable for you" and bring structures — tier down with a
documented path up, extended payment terms, a shorter term, a pause with data retained.
Optimise for keeping the logo. See `churn-risk` Budget squeeze.

### "Your competitor quoted 40% less."

**Underneath:** either a real quote, an anchoring tactic, or a genuinely different scope.

Ask what is in their scope. Most cheaper quotes are cheaper because they are smaller. Compare
on total cost including implementation, integration, and the internal effort of switching.
Do not disparage the competitor — it reads as insecurity and is remembered.

**If it is a real like-for-like gap:** say so honestly and make the case on switching cost,
risk, and what they would lose. If there is no case, that is information you needed.

## Value and ROI

### "We're not sure we're getting value."

**Underneath:** usually true, and usually because no baseline was ever captured.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Answer structure** | Do not defend. Agree to find out, and be specific about how. "Let's establish what 'value' means here — what would have to be true at renewal for this to be obviously worth it?" |
| **Evidence ready** | Whatever baseline exists; usage by team; outcomes achieved; the success plan and its status |
| **If pressed** | Propose a value review with a defined method and date. See `success-plan` §baseline capture. |
| **Never say** | "But your usage is up 30%" — usage is not value, and answering value with usage is the single clearest rookie tell |

### "Can you prove the ROI?"

Bring the arithmetic, the assumptions, and the sensitivity. A defensible ROI states its
assumptions out loud and shows what happens if they are wrong. See `qbr-builder`
§value-realization.

The strongest version of this answer is not our number — it is **their** number. "Your team
told us in March that this saved roughly six hours a week across eleven people. At your
loaded cost that's about $X a year. Does that match what you'd say internally?"

### "Our team says they could do this in a spreadsheet."

Sometimes true. Ask what would break at their volume, what happens when the person who owns
the spreadsheet leaves, and who audits it. If none of those matter at their scale, they may
be right — and knowing that early is better than losing the renewal to it.

## Competitive

### "We're evaluating alternatives."

**Underneath:** either genuine, or a negotiation lever, or a decision already made.

Ask to be part of the evaluation properly: what are the criteria, who decides, by when. A
customer who will not share the criteria has usually already decided. Never respond by
discounting — it confirms the price was wrong all along.

### "Company X has feature Y."

Confirm they need Y before defending. Most feature comparisons are checklist artifacts from a
vendor's own battlecard rather than real requirements. If they do need it: say whether we have
it, will have it, or will not — and never invent a date.

## Product gaps

### "You still haven't shipped the thing we asked for."

**Underneath:** a broken expectation, which damages trust more than the missing feature does.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Answer structure** | Acknowledge the request and its date. State the real status. If the answer is no, say no. |
| **Evidence ready** | The request, its date, its Jira/Linear status, and the actual product decision |
| **If pressed** | Offer the workaround, an escalation path to product, or an honest no with the reasoning. |
| **Never say** | A roadmap date you do not own. This is the single most common way a CSM converts a product gap into a churn. |

A clear "no, and here's why, and here's what we can do instead" preserves more trust than a
soft "it's on the roadmap" that turns out to be false.

### "It's too hard to use."

Ask for the specific workflow. "Hard to use" is almost always one or two steps, not the whole
product. Bring the usage data by feature — you can often name the step before they do, which
demonstrates you have been paying attention.

## Trust and reliability

### "We've had too many outages / bugs."

Bring the actual incident record — dates, durations, root causes, what changed. Do not
minimise. If the record is genuinely bad, say so; a customer who has lived through it will
not accept a rosier version and will stop believing everything else you say.

Then: what has changed structurally, with evidence. Not "we take reliability seriously".

### "Support takes too long."

Bring their actual numbers: first response, resolution, reopen rate, compared against their
own history and against the SLA. If the numbers are bad, lead with that. If they are good but
the perception is bad, the gap is usually one memorable ticket — find it and address that one.

### "You promised X and it didn't happen."

Verify before responding — check the commitment log. If it is true, acknowledge it plainly and
say what changed. If the record shows something different, share the record without arguing
about memory: "here's what I have from the 12 June note; help me see where we diverged."

## Adoption and change

### "Our team won't use it."

Ask which team and what they use instead. Adoption failures are usually workflow conflicts
rather than product objections. Bring usage by team so the conversation is about the specific
group that stalled, not about "the team".

### "We don't have time to implement this properly."

Real, and usually the honest reason behind a stalled rollout. Offer to reduce the scope of
what "properly" means — one team, one workflow, one measurable outcome — rather than pushing
the full plan against a constraint that will not move.

## Organisational

### "We're restructuring / there's a new CIO / we've been acquired."

Do not sell. Establish the new decision-making structure and who owns the relationship now.
Assume you are being re-evaluated whether or not anyone says so. See `churn-risk`
Regime change and Consolidation target.

### "The person who bought this has left."

Assume zero inherited context and zero inherited goodwill. Rebuild the business case with the
successor as if it were a new sale, and do not open by asking who replaced them — see
`intervention-plays` §champion recovery.

## The questions nobody prepares for

These are the ones that end meetings badly, and they are the ones most briefs omit.

| Question | The only good answer |
| --- | --- |
| **"Why should we renew?"** | Asked directly, this means the value case has not landed. Have a three-sentence answer built on their stated objective and a number they recognise. If you cannot produce one, that is the finding, and the meeting should be about building it. |
| **"What would you do if you were me?"** | Answer honestly, including the case for spending less with you. The credibility earned by an honest answer usually exceeds the revenue at stake in the question. |
| **"Is this the best price you can do?"** | "It's the right price for this scope. If the budget is the constraint, let's change the scope or the structure rather than the rate." |
| **"Can you guarantee that?"** | Never guarantee. Commit to what you control, name the owner, and name the date. |
| **"Are you going to be acquired / are you running out of money?"** | Answer within what you are permitted to say, and do not evade. Evasion confirms the fear. |
| **"Why is your team so hard to reach?"** | Check the record before answering — response latency is measurable. If it is true, say so and fix it structurally. |
| **"We're only renewing for one year this time."** | Do not celebrate the renewal. A shortened term is a Contraction-spiral component and the risk record opens now. |

## Preparing objections for a specific call

For every brief, prepare **at least three**, and one of them must be uncomfortable. The three
to prepare are, in order:

1. The one their **data** predicts — the usage decline, the open P1, the missed milestone.
2. The one their **role** predicts — a CFO asks about cost, a CIO about risk and consolidation, a practitioner about workflow.
3. The one you are **hoping they will not ask** — write it down, because that is the one that
   costs you the meeting if it arrives unprepared.
