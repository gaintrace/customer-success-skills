# Running the Room

> Read this before the meeting, not after it. The deck is built by then; what is left is the
> forty-five minutes in which a decision either gets made or does not.

## 1. The first ninety seconds

The room decides in the first ninety seconds whether this is a meeting or a presentation.

**Open with the decision, not with thanks.** "We're here to agree three things and to get
your answer on one: whether Legal joins the rollout this quarter. I'll take twenty minutes,
you take the rest." That sentence buys the next forty-three minutes.

**Round-table introductions are a budget item.** Presented minutes are budgeted at 21 of 45.
Seven people introducing themselves eat a meaningful share of that before anything is
decided. Put names, roles and reason-for-attending on the invite, and open on the purpose.
Where someone genuinely is new, introduce them in one line yourself.

**Say who is missing and why it matters**, once, at the start — not discovered at slide 9
when the person who owns the decision turns out to be on leave.

**Set the interruption rule explicitly.** "Stop me anywhere — the discussion is the point."
Without it, executives save their questions and then run out of time to ask them.

## 2. Opening with the miss

The miss is slide 4 and it goes **before** the value slide (**C29**, **R20**). Heard from you
first, everything after it is credible; raised by them, everything before it was marketing.
Where any success-plan milestone was missed this slide is required — a deck without it is not a
shorter deck, it is a false one, and the skill does not emit it.

**Structure, in this order:**

1. **What happened** — plainly, in one sentence, with the date.
2. **What it cost them**, in their units — hours, delayed close, tickets, a missed internal
   commitment of theirs. Never in our units.
3. **Why** — the actual reason, not the process narrative.
4. **What we already changed** — past tense, already done, verifiable.
5. **What we commit to** — owner, date, and what it produces.
6. **How they will be able to tell** — the observable, so the commitment is checkable.

**Never pair a miss with a "but".** "We missed the September date, but adoption was strong"
reads as deflection and costs more than the miss did. Pair the miss with a date instead.

**One slide, five minutes, cap of three rows.** More than three and this is an escalation
review, not a business review — say so out loud and re-book the value conversation. Trying
to run both in one meeting fails at both.

**Order is fixed: our misses → their blockers → external factors.** Reversing it reads as
blame-shifting even when it is accurate.

**Do not apologise twice for the same thing.** A second apology asks the customer to absolve
you, which makes your feelings their job.

**Never attach a commercial ask to the miss** (**R11**), and never let the value number arrive
in the same breath as the apology. The miss is closed with a date, then the deck moves on.

## 2b. Pre-wiring the decisions

Nothing should be decided for the first time in a group meeting (**C9**). Every attendee's
position on every decision the review must reach is known beforehand, and the meeting ratifies
rather than debates.

| Status | What it means | What the plan does with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **WIRED** | Spoken to individually, position recorded verbatim with the date, no objection open | Item stays on the agenda and is read back for ratification |
| **PARTIAL** | Spoken to; a specific objection is still open | The objection, its owner and its resolution date go on the agenda beside the item |
| **UNWIRED** | Not spoken to, or their position is unknown | **The deck is not emitted with this on it.** Pre-wire by `meeting − 3 business days`, or drop the item and record that you dropped it |

The pre-wire call is two questions long: *"Before I put this in front of the group — where do
you land on it, and what would you need to be comfortable saying yes in the room?"* An
objection heard in private is a conversation; the same objection heard in the group is a
verdict, because the person now has an audience for it.

**Dropping an unwired item is the recommendation, not the fallback.** An item debated live
consumes the discussion minutes the whole deck was budgeted around, and it usually ends in
"let us take that away", which is nothing.

## 3. The champion pre-call

Twenty minutes, five to seven days ahead. The highest-return part of the whole process:
it converts every surprise in the room into a surprise in a private call where it can be
fixed.

**The five questions**, in this order:

| # | Question | What a good answer sounds like | What to do with a bad one |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "Here is the figure I'm putting on screen and its inputs — will your finance team accept these, and which would they challenge first?" | They name the input, not the total | Fix the number, not the slide. A champion who will not defend the figure privately will not defend it publicly |
| 2 | **"Say it back to me — what number would you put on this?"** | A figure in *their* words, which you write down verbatim with the date | Without it the benefit line is `vendor-asserted` and cannot lead the slide (**C5**). Ask what they would need in order to put a number on it, and make that the ask |
| 3 | "Here is the miss I'm opening with — is there a second one I haven't seen?" | A second miss, usually smaller and older | Add it. A miss they raise in the room that you skipped costs more than both misses combined |
| 4 | "Who is actually attending, and what does each of them need to hear?" | Names, plus one line each on what they care about | If the economic buyer is not attending an EBR, re-book rather than downgrade in place |
| 5 | **"When your VP asks what changed this quarter, what do you want to be able to say your team did?"** | A specific thing their team built, fixed or ran — checkable by their VP | Without it there is no credit line, and the champion's one-pager is not emitted at all (**C20**). Keep asking until you have something specific |

**Question 2 is the highest-leverage sentence in the process.** An ROI figure you assert is a
vendor claim; the same figure in their words is internal evidence they will repeat in a budget
meeting you are not in. Log it verbatim, with the speaker and the date, into the Customer-stated
column of the value case.

**Question 5 produces the credit line**, which is what makes the champion's one-pager worth
sending. People advocate internally for things that make them look good; a page with no win in
it for the champion gets sent once and never again.

**Exit criteria.** The champion has agreed to: the value figure, the three goals, the attendee
list and the miss — **and has supplied one quotable number and one credit line**. Record all six
as ticks in Part A7 of the internal plan.

**Nothing appears in the room that the champion has not seen.** A number that surprises the
champion gets contested rather than accepted, and you lose the one person who was going to
carry it internally.

**Ask what they want to look good doing.** The champion is spending internal credibility on
this meeting. Knowing which part of it they want to own — the value figure, the roadmap
alignment, the goal for their own team — lets you hand it to them deliberately.

## 4. Challenge handling

| Challenge | Poor answer | Better answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Where did this baseline come from?" | "From our data" | "Your close calendar, extracted by your FP&A team on 30 November — J. Alvarez sent it over. Does that match your recollection?" |
| "That seems high." | Defending the total | "Which input would you move? At α = 0.5 rather than 0.7 it is 1.0× instead of 1.34×, and I would still be here" |
| "We did a lot of that ourselves." | "It's a joint effort" | "Agreed — that is why the attribution factor is 0.7 and not 1.0, and here are the two confounders we haircut for" |
| "Usage looks low to me." | Explaining the metric | "It is low in Legal and it should be. Finance went 4 → 22. Here is the split by team" |
| "Your competitor does X." | Rebutting the competitor | "Tell me what X would let you do that you can't today — I want to know whether that gap is real for you" |
| "We're looking at cutting tools next year." | Panic, discount, or a save pitch | "Then let's make sure the case is written in your terms before that review. What does it have to say?" |
| "Can you just send the deck?" | Sending it | "I'll send it — and the twenty minutes I want is for the two decisions, not the slides. Which two weeks work?" |
| "Why are we only hearing about this now?" | Explaining the process | "Because we should have told you in June. Here is what changed so that the next one reaches you in the week it happens" |
| Silence at a stop-and-ask point | Moving on | "That silence usually means the slide isn't about you. What would be on it if you'd built it?" |

**The rule underneath all of these:** answer the challenge with the input, not with the
conclusion. A challenged total is a debate; a challenged input is a conversation.

## 5. The commitment ladder

Not all yeses are the same, and hearing the difference is the skill. Rank what you actually
got, in the internal plan, using this ladder.

| Rung | What was said | Worth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | A named person states a specific action with a date, unprompted | Real. Log it |
| 4 | A named person accepts an action with a date when asked directly | Real. Log it and confirm in the recap |
| 3 | "Yes, we should do that" with no owner named | Not a commitment. Ask for the name before moving on |
| 2 | Nodding, "makes sense", "leave it with us" | Nothing. Convert or drop |
| 1 | Silence after a name is spoken | A no. Treat it as one |

**Convert upward in the room, not in the follow-up email.** "Leave it with us" becomes a
commitment by asking one question: *"Who on your side should I put against that, and is the
14th realistic?"* Trying to convert it afterwards by writing it into the recap as though it
was agreed is how the next review starts with an argument.

## 6. The close

Three moves, two minutes. It is the part that gets skipped when the meeting runs long, which
is why the drop order protects it and cuts evidence instead.

1. **Read back.** "The three things we agreed are…" — said out loud, with an audible yes
   from a named person. Not "I'll send a summary".
2. **Assign.** Say the owner's name on their side for each one. Silence is a no; treat it as
   a no in the internal plan even if it felt polite.
3. **Date it.** Propose a specific date and time before anyone leaves. "I'll follow up" is
   not a date, and the calendar is the only artifact that survives the week.

**In an EBR, ask the renewal-intent question and log the answer verbatim:**

> "Is there anything you can see between now and `<opt-out date>` that would stop this
> continuing?"

Ask it against the **opt-out deadline** (`renewal_date − notice_period_days`), never the
renewal date. With 90 days' notice, a 1 February renewal is decided in October.

Log what they actually said, not your interpretation. "Nothing I can think of" and "not from
my side, but procurement is reviewing everything" are different answers with different
follow-ups, and the paraphrase loses exactly the part that mattered.

**Never close on a thank-you slide.** Slide 10 is the ask. Gratitude is a sentence, not a
slot in a 45-minute meeting.

## 7. Post-review measurement

Measure the review, not the ceremony. "The QBR happened" is attendance, not outcome.

| # | Measure | Definition | Target | Window |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Commitment capture rate** | Commitments with an owner *and* a date ÷ all commitments made | 100% | At recap |
| 2 | **Our completion rate** | Our commitments completed by their stated date ÷ our commitments | ≥90% `[R]` | Next review |
| 3 | **Goal attainment** | Goals met ÷ goals set | ≥60% `[R]` | Next review |
| 4 | **Economic buyer attendance (EBR)** | EBRs with the economic buyer present ÷ EBRs held | ≥80% `[R]` | Rolling 4 quarters |
| 5 | **Decision rate** | Reviews producing the named decision ÷ reviews held | ≥75% `[R]` | Rolling 4 quarters |
| 6 | **Buying-team usage delta** | Active users in the buyer's team, 30 days after vs 30 days before | Positive | T+30 |
| 7 | **Talk-time ratio** | Our speaking minutes ÷ meeting minutes | ≤0.5 | Per review |
| 8 | **Pre-call coverage** | Reviews with a completed champion pre-call ÷ reviews held | 100% | Per review |
| 9 | **Pre-wire coverage** | Decisions with every needed position WIRED before the deck was built ÷ decisions taken to the room | 100% | Per review |
| 10 | **Customer-stated coverage** | Benefit lines carrying a quote, speaker and date ÷ benefit lines presented | ≥1 on the headline line, always | Per review |
| 11 | **One-pager send rate** | Reviews where the champion's internal one-pager was sent ÷ reviews held | 100% | Per review |

`[R]` marks a starting-point target, not a measured benchmark. Replace each with your own
observed rate after one full cycle; a target you inherited and never checked is worse than
no target.

**Do not claim a review caused a renewal.** The comparison between accounts that hold
reviews and accounts that do not is confounded by selection in both directions: healthy
accounts accept reviews, and unhealthy ones decline them. Report attendance, decision rate
and goal attainment. Attributing the renewal to the meeting is the kind of claim that gets
CS budgets cut when someone finally checks it.

## 8. Keeping the room after bad news

Sometimes the review opens into something larger: an outage they are still angry about, a
departure, a budget cut, an evaluation of an alternative.

| Situation | The move | The mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| They arrive angry | Reflect their words back accurately before responding at all. Then ask what they want from the next forty minutes | Running the agenda anyway |
| A champion has left | Stop and ask who is picking up the work. The rest of the deck is built for an audience that is no longer there | Presenting a deck aimed at someone who is gone |
| Budget cut announced in the room | Ask what has to be true for this to survive, and write it down as the goal | Discounting on the spot |
| They name an alternative they are evaluating | "I'd rather be part of that conversation than outside it." Ask what it would have to do | Rebutting the alternative feature by feature |
| An escalation dominates | Say it out loud: this is an escalation review now. Cover the escalation properly, re-book the value conversation with a date | Squeezing both in and doing neither |
| The exec halves the meeting | Run the pre-written 15-minute cut. Say what you are dropping and when it will arrive | Improvising, and dropping the ask instead of the evidence |
| Nobody speaks | Ask the stop-and-ask question and wait through the silence. Count to seven | Filling the silence yourself |

**The meeting after the bad meeting matters more.** Whatever happened, the follow-up goes
out within 24 business hours with every commitment carrying an owner and a date, and the
next date already proposed. A review that went badly and was followed up precisely recovers;
one that went well and drifted does not.
