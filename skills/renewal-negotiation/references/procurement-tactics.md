## 1. What they are measured on

Not "getting the price down" in the abstract. Four things, and knowing which one is live changes the
whole conversation.

| Measured on | What it means in the room | What satisfies it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Savings against a baseline** | They must be able to report a number to their CFO | A documented saving of any kind — waived services, extended payment terms, a bundled module, a shorter path to volume pricing. It does **not** have to be the subscription rate |
| **Cycle time** | Deals that sit in their queue are their problem too | A complete pack on day one; a named owner on our side; pre-agreed redlines |
| **Risk terms accepted** | Liability, data protection, security, insurance, exit rights | A standard MSA that has already been accepted by comparable buyers, and a straight answer on what you will not move |
| **Compliance and process integrity** | The steps must be seen to have happened | Not skipping their process, even when the business owner wants to |

**The strategic consequence.** If they must report a saving and the only number on the table is the
subscription rate, then the subscription rate is where the saving comes from. **Give them something
else to save against, early.** A quote that includes services, training and a support tier gives them
three places to find a number that does not come out of the recurring base.

**Who actually decides.** Procurement rarely owns the decision to keep the product; they own the terms
on which it is kept. The business owner decides. If you have no route to the business owner except
through procurement, that is the problem to solve first — and it is a relationship problem, not a
pricing one (`R5`).

## 2. The tactics

| # | The move | The tell | The counter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Early engagement, T-90 or earlier** | A procurement contact appears with a checklist and a timeline | This is the healthy case. Give everything at once: current pricing and usage, entitlement report, security package, insurance certificates, entity and tax data, and the redlines already accepted elsewhere. Early engagement is documented to compress the contract phase `[V]` |
| 2 | **Late entry, inside T-30** | Silence, then a new name on the thread with a hard date | Name the paper path and its measured lead times. Propose a 30–60 day extension at current terms (§6). The extension removes the artificial deadline, and it is a genuine option — not a bluff |
| 3 | **Benchmark claim** — "we have quotes 30% lower" | A percentage with no document behind it | §3. Scope-normalise first; never match a number whose scope you have not seen |
| 4 | **Best-and-final at T-14** | "Send your best and final by Friday" | A best-and-final is conditional on a named signature date and a named get, or it is simply a lower price. Reply with both attached |
| 5 | **Manufactured deadline** | A date that appears in no contract and no board calendar | Anchor on `D`, which is verifiable in their own paper. Ask, without edge: "help me understand the date — our contractual decision point is 3 November" |
| 6 | **Splitting the CSM from the AE** | Separate calls, each asking what the other said | §4. Structural counter, not a clever answer |
| 7 | **Escalation over the account team** | A note to your VP or CRO | Expected and fine — provided your exec has already met theirs. A first contact made under pressure is worth nothing |
| 8 | **Last-minute term demands** | MFN, unlimited liability, TfC, uncapped SLA credits, arriving at T-7 | Late-arriving terms go to the never-concede register by default, not to legal. "That one isn't a pricing decision" is a complete answer |
| 9 | **Salami slicing** | A sequence of small asks after each agreement | Bundle: "let's put everything outstanding on one list and close it together." Never re-open a closed item |
| 10 | **The silent three weeks** | No reply after an active period | More often a reorganisation than a tactic. Ask the champion, not procurement, and re-confirm `D` in writing |
| 11 | **Unbundling to compare** | "Price each module separately" | Provide it, and price the bundle discount explicitly so the bundle's value is visible rather than assumed |
| 12 | **The consolidation review** | "We're rationalising the vendor estate" | This is a portfolio decision, not an account decision. Escalate to the exec sponsor the same week; compete on switching cost and integration depth, never on price |

**On tactic 5 — and the mirror.** Buyer-side advisers describe vendors creating urgency with
time-limited offers, and buyers doing the same in reverse `[V]`. **Do neither.** A manufactured
deadline is the cheapest possible short-term lever and the most expensive long-term one: a customer
who catches you inventing urgency re-prices the credibility of everything you have ever told them,
including the value numbers you spent a quarter building.

## 3. The competitive quote

The most common single tactic, and the one most often mishandled. It is frequently *true* — of a
different scope.

**Do not ask "can you share the quote?"** They usually cannot. Ask for a scope comparison instead,
which they can give and which does the same work:

| Ask | Why |
| --- | --- |
| "What's included at that number — which modules, how many seats, what term?" | Most gaps appear here |
| "Does it include implementation, migration and training, or are those separate?" | Frequently separate, and frequently large |
| "What support tier is in it, and what's the SLA?" | Often the difference alone |
| "Is that a first-year price or the steady-state price?" | Land-and-expand pricing is designed to compare well in year one |
| "What happens to it in year two?" | The escalator is often absent from the comparison |

Then bring **their** switching cost — computed from their numbers, not a market average (Step 1 of
`../SKILL.md`, worked in `deal-structures.md` §6): integrations to rebuild, records under management,
trained users to retrain, the parallel-run period, and the reports and approvals to reconstruct.
State it once, plainly, and stop. Repeating it turns a fact into a threat.

**The price-match rule.** Match a number only where you have seen its scope, and only as a
term-limited credit with written snap-back — never as a base reprice. A matched price with no
snap-back is a permanent reprice bought with a document you never read.

**When the alternative is genuinely better for them**, say so and manage the exit (`R21`). It costs
one renewal and preserves the reference and the win-back. A fought exit costs both.

## 4. Splitting the CSM from the AE

The most effective tactic used against vendor teams, because it exploits a real structural weakness:
the CSM wants the relationship to survive and the AE wants the number, and those two wants can be
played against each other by anyone patient enough to make two phone calls.

**How it runs:** separate conversations, each opening with a version of "your colleague indicated
there might be some flexibility". Neither statement is checkable in the moment, and the CSM — whose
instinct is to be helpful — supplies the flexibility.

**The counter is structural, not rhetorical.**

| Rule | In practice |
| --- | --- |
| **The RM or AE owns price; the CSM owns value** `[P]` | A CSM who negotiates price cannot be the customer's advocate at the next renewal, and both jobs are worse for the merge |
| **They are not both in the pricing conversation** | Two people in the room is two chances to be split |
| **Neither prices anything outside a scheduled conversation** | No hallway numbers, no numbers in a Slack Connect channel, no numbers in a reply sent from a phone |
| **Say the split to the customer, plainly and without drama** | "Priya owns commercial terms; I'll stay on the outcomes side." It is honest, it is normal, and it removes the ambiguity the tactic needs |
| **One written record of every number offered** | The concession log (`concession-ladder.md` §9). If it is not in the log, it was not offered |

**Where one person holds both roles** — common below enterprise — run the two conversations on
different days, and open each by saying which one it is. "Today is the value review; we'll do
commercial terms on Thursday." That single sentence recovers most of the benefit of the split.

## 4b. Re-opening a business thread

The moment a procurement address appears, start a clock. **`C10`: procurement may be a live thread;
it must never be the only one.** When the only conversation running is commercial, price is the only
variable left, and the value case cannot be made in a thread whose subject line is a quote number.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **The measure** | `days_since_business_thread` — today minus the last conversation about outcomes, adoption, roadmap, support or the success plan, with a counterpart **outside procurement and legal**. `UNKNOWN` counts as over the limit |
| **The limit** | 21 days while procurement is active. Past it, Gate B fails and no rung is offered (`concession-ladder.md` §0) |
| **The unblock** | A dated, non-commercial meeting with the economic buyer. It has to be **booked**, not held, before the commercial reply goes out |
| **Copy procurement in** | The rule is a second thread, not a secret one. Routing around procurement is the one move that reliably makes their process slower |

**Three plays**, in full with their disqualifiers in `concession-ladder.md` §0, send-ready as Draft 0
of `../assets/negotiation-comms.md`: **the delta** (a team's usage has moved measurably), **the open
milestone** (a success-plan item is due, slipped or has landed), **the shipped ask** (something they
asked for has shipped).

**What makes procurement comfortable with it.** Say what you are doing, in the commercial thread, in
one line: *"I've asked Dana for thirty minutes on the ops numbers — separate conversation, nothing to
do with the paperwork, and you're copied."* A procurement lead objects to being bypassed, not to the
business owner being spoken to. Bypassing them is what produces the slow process everyone then
complains about.

## 5. Paper lead times

A renewal agreed in principle still has to pass through their machinery, and the machinery has its own
clock. This is where renewals that were never at commercial risk slip a quarter.

| Workstream | Triggered by | Who owns it on their side |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Security re-review / questionnaire | Annual policy, a new CISO, or any change of scope | Security or IT risk |
| Legal redlines | Any change to the MSA or a new order form | Legal or outside counsel |
| Privacy / DPIA / sub-processor review | New data flows, new region, new sub-processor | Privacy office or DPO |
| Vendor master and tax setup | Any change of entity, bank details or billing address | AP / finance operations |
| Insurance certificates | Annual, or on request | Risk |
| Budget approval | Amount crossing an approval threshold — including one crossed *by the uplift* | Business owner, then finance |
| PO issuance | After budget approval, before invoicing | Procurement operations |
| Signature routing | After everything else | Whoever holds signing authority for the amount |

**This library ships no default durations, deliberately.** A security review takes four days at one
customer and nine weeks at another; a default schedules a false sense of safety. Where you have not
measured, write `UNKNOWN — requires cycle time from prior renewals`, exclude it from the critical
path, and report the path as a **floor**.

**Measure your own**, from your last ten renewals: the date each workstream was first requested and
the date it completed. Plan against the **80th percentile**, not the median — the median plan misses
half the time, which is not a plan. The backward arithmetic from `D` belongs to `renewal-prep` §8 and
`../../renewal-prep/scripts/renewal_calendar.py`; this file only insists that the numbers be yours.

**The threshold nobody checks:** an uplift can push the total across an approval tier that the flat
renewal would not have crossed, adding an approver and weeks of cycle time. Check the tier before you
choose the rung, not after.

## 5b. The statutory position

Auto-renewal and notice mechanics are a legal question, not a negotiating one. Read this before
answering a notice question, then **route it to counsel** — "the clause says so" is not the end of
the argument, and it is not a position you want to be discovering in front of the customer.

| Jurisdiction / instrument | What it does | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **New York GOL §5-903** | Makes an automatic-renewal provision in a covered service contract unenforceable by the provider unless written notice is served 15–30 days before the deadline for the recipient to give notice. It expressly reaches **business** recipients as well as individuals, for contracts whose renewal period exceeds one month | In force [NY General Obligations Law §5-903 · statute text · 2026] |
| **US FTC "click-to-cancel" / revised Negative Option Rule** | Would have imposed disclosure, consent and simple-cancellation requirements on negative-option offers, including B2B | **Vacated** by the Eighth Circuit on 8 July 2025 [law-firm client alerts, 2025 · `[V]`] |
| **ROSCA and FTC Act §5** | Survive the vacatur: clear disclosure of material terms before billing information is taken, express informed consent, and a simple cancellation mechanism | In force [law-firm client alerts, 2025–2026 · `[V]`] |
| **FTC rulemaking, January 2026** | An Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on negative-option practices was submitted 30 January 2026 | Pending [law-firm client alerts, 2026 · `[V]`] |
| **Other US states and non-US jurisdictions** | Several states have their own automatic-renewal statutes with differing notice windows and coverage; non-US positions differ again | `UNKNOWN — requires counsel for the governing law on this contract` |

**What this means operationally**, and it is the same answer whatever counsel says about the statute:

1. **Send the courtesy notice-deadline letter at T-120 and again at T-90**, naming the date in
   writing. It costs nothing, it is compliant everywhere, and it removes the question entirely.
2. **Never build a renewal plan on the customer missing their own notice window.** Even where the
   clause is enforceable, winning a term the customer did not intend to buy costs the next two
   renewals, and it is the churn nobody will explain to you honestly.
3. **State the opt-out date in the body of any email that concerns price.** A customer who learns the
   date from you trusts the number that comes with it.
4. **Where the governing law is not the one you assumed** — a subsidiary, a different signing entity,
   a non-US parent — write `UNKNOWN — requires counsel` and stop, rather than reasoning from the US
   position by analogy.

This section is a prompt to ask counsel, not legal advice, and nothing in it is a substitute for the
governing law of the specific contract.

## 6. The extension

The most under-used instrument in a late renewal, and the honest answer to a manufactured deadline.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it is** | A 30–60 day extension of the current agreement at current terms, so the decision is made on the merits rather than under a clock |
| **When to propose it** | Procurement enters inside T-30; the serial paper path exceeds the days remaining to `D`; or a security or legal review lands late through nobody's fault |
| **When not to** | As a way to avoid a decision that has already been made, or repeatedly — a second extension is a signal that something else is wrong |
| **What to ask for with it** | A named signature date at the end of it. An extension with no date attached is a deferral, not a plan |
| **How to say it** | "We're not going to get security, legal and the PO done properly by 3 November. I'd rather extend the current terms 45 days and do it properly than have either of us make a rushed call. Can we agree 18 December as the signature date?" |
| **What it protects** | Their notice deadline and yours. Both sides keep their options, which is precisely why it disarms an artificial date |

Prepare the extension **before** it is needed, with the paper already drafted. An extension improvised
at T-7 reads as panic; the same document offered at T-45 reads as competence.

## 7. Working with them well

The counters above are defensive. Most of the value is in the offensive half, which is simply being
easy to buy from.

| Move | Why it pays |
| --- | --- |
| **Send the complete pack on day one** | Pricing, usage and entitlement report, security package, insurance certificates, entity and tax data, standard redlines. Their cycle-time metric improves, and yours does too |
| **Name one owner on our side** | Every question routes to one person with a stated response time. Ambiguity on our side is a delay on theirs |
| **Volunteer the notice deadline** | It costs nothing, it is in their contract anyway, and it converts you into the vendor who does not play games |
| **Pre-agree the redlines** | Keep a list of the positions already accepted by comparable buyers and offer it unprompted |
| **Give them a saving that is not the rate** | Waived onboarding, bundled training, extended terms, a longer volume tier. A reportable number that does not touch the recurring base |
| **Answer "no" plainly** | "That one I can't do at any level" is more useful to a procurement professional than a maybe. They have to plan too |
| **Never surprise them** | An increase they hear first from their business owner is an increase they will fight on principle |

## 8. Failure modes

| Failure | What it looks like | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Treating procurement as the enemy | Routing around them to the business owner | They will find out, and their process will slow to match |
| Treating procurement as the decision-maker | Negotiating value with someone who is not measured on value | Value goes to the business owner; terms go to procurement |
| Matching an unseen quote | "We'll meet the 30%" | Scope-normalise, then price switching cost from their numbers |
| Answering a deadline you cannot verify | Discounting to hit a date that exists nowhere | Anchor on `D`, offer the extension |
| Two people pricing | CSM and AE both quoting in separate calls | One owner of price; one written log |
| A number floated before approval | "I think we can probably do ten" | It is now the floor. Never float an unapproved number |
| Late terms sent to legal | An MFN clause at T-7 going into review | Never-concede register first; legal only sees what is negotiable |
| Inventing a lead time | "Security usually takes a week" | Measure your own; otherwise `UNKNOWN` and report the path as a floor |
| Improvised extension at T-7 | A panicked request for more time | Draft it at T-45 and hold it ready |
| Manufactured urgency of our own | "This pricing expires Friday" | Banned. The credibility cost outlives the deal |
